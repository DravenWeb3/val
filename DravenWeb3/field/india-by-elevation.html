Why this exists

The highest ground, everywhere.

A "highest point" isn't always a Himalayan giant — for six states and UTs it's a coastal ridge under 1,000m, and for a couple of union territories it's barely above sea level. What follows is the full list, no state or territory skipped: 28 states, 8 union territories, 36 highpoints, from a 15-metre atoll rise in Lakshadweep to Kangchenjunga's 8,586m summit on the Sikkim–Nepal border.

The data below is compiled from state gazetteers, Survey of India references, and peak-record sources. A few figures are disputed by a matter of metres depending on source — noted where relevant. Some entries are technical, permit-gated, high-altitude expeditions (Ladakh, J&K, Nanda Devi, Kangchenjunga); most are accessible day hikes.

DRAVEN · FIELD NOTES

Elevation figures are drawn from state gazetteers, Survey of India references, and peak-record sources (Peakbagger, state tourism boards, Wikipedia's state-highpoint compilation). A handful of highpoints are disputed by 20–50m between sources, and several — Girnar, Karoh Peak, Dhupgarh among them — have been revised by later surveys; treat these as best-available, not laser-measured.

Restricted terrain: Saltoro Kangri (Ladakh) sits inside the Siachen military zone and is not open to civilian climbers. Nanda Devi's core sanctuary has been closed to climbing since 1983 for ecological protection — only outer buffer-zone treks (Nanda Devi East base, Milam) are permitted. Kangchenjunga's true summit is traditionally left unclimbed by mountaineers out of respect for Sikkimese religious belief. Kangto (Arunachal) and Nun Peak (J&K) sit near international borders and require Inner Line / Protected Area permits arranged well in advance.
