ZONE 1

Foundational L1s

The base settlement layers everything else is built on top of. Ranked here by structural role, not by price — this is a map of what each chain is for, not a trading signal. Ten chains that between them define the design space: store-of-value, programmable settlement, high-throughput execution, and the interoperability layer connecting all of it.
Bitcoin (BTC)
Core Infra
launched 2009 · Nakamoto consensus, Proof-of-Work · ~10-min block time, finality in 30–60 min
The reference implementation of a trustless, permissionless ledger — still the largest by market capitalization and the asset institutional capital treats as digital gold rather than a transactional currency. Its own base layer intentionally does very little; everything programmable happens either off-chain (Lightning Network) or on separate execution layers that settle back to it.
Ethereum (ETH)
Core Infra
launched 2015 · Proof-of-Stake since 2022's Merge · ~12-second blocks, economic finality in minutes
The dominant smart-contract platform — still anchors more than half of all on-chain stablecoin supply and the largest share of DeFi total value locked of any base layer, even as day-to-day transaction volume has migrated to its own Layer 2 rollup ecosystem (Zone 2). EIP-4844 and successive upgrades were built specifically to make that L2 migration cheaper, positioning Ethereum's own base layer increasingly as a settlement and data-availability layer rather than where most execution happens.
Solana (SOL)
Scaling / Performance
launched 2020 · Proof-of-History + Proof-of-Stake hybrid · sub-second block times, sub-cent fees
Built around maximizing throughput and minimizing cost by innovating at the consensus layer rather than pushing execution to a separate layer, the opposite architectural bet from Ethereum's rollup-centric roadmap. The 2026 Firedancer client rollout added a second independent validator implementation, reducing single-client risk that had previously caused network outages. Became the default venue for high-frequency retail and speculative flow precisely because of that speed and cost profile.
BNB Chain
Scaling / Performance
operated in connection with the Binance exchange ecosystem · EVM-compatible
Optimized for cheap, high-volume retail flow and tightly integrated with the world's largest crypto exchange by trading volume — a more centralized trust model than Ethereum's in exchange for consistently low fees, which made it a major venue for the same memecoin/speculative-flow cycle Solana captured.
XRP Ledger
Financial Primitive
launched 2012, built by Ripple Labs · designed for cross-border payment settlement
Purpose-built for fast, low-cost value transfer between financial institutions rather than general-purpose smart contracts. Ripple's multi-year SEC litigation over whether XRP constitutes a security was a defining regulatory case for the whole industry; its resolution and the subsequent launch of spot XRP ETFs reinforced the ledger's positioning specifically around real-world payment utility rather than DeFi or NFT activity.
Cosmos
Core Infra
the "Internet of Blockchains" · IBC protocol live across 100+ independent chains
Not a single chain but a framework (Cosmos SDK + Tendermint/CometBFT consensus) for building fully sovereign, independently-secured "zones" that interoperate through the Inter-Blockchain Communication protocol. Each chain bootstraps and secures its own validator set — full autonomy, at the cost of needing to attract that validator set from scratch. Osmosis, dYdX, and Celestia are among its best-known independent chains; dYdX's migration from Ethereum specifically for execution control is the clearest case study in why teams choose this model.
Polkadot
Core Infra
Layer-0 multichain protocol · Relay Chain + parachains, shared security model
Takes the opposite design bet from Cosmos: parachains connect to a central Relay Chain and inherit its validation directly, trading full chain-level autonomy for not having to bootstrap independent security. Over 65 parachains (Acala, Moonbeam, Astar among them) run on this shared-security model. A 2026 tokenomics overhaul cut annual DOT issuance and introduced a hard 2.1 billion supply cap — a governance-driven monetary policy change voted through Polkadot's own OpenGov system.
Avalanche
Scaling / Performance
Subnets architecture · EVM-compatible, sub-second finality
Lets teams spin up independent "subnets" — application-specific chains with total autonomy over their own rules, public or private — connected back to the primary network through cryptographically-proven messaging rather than a shared relay chain. The framework most optimized for deployment speed among the three major interoperability architectures (alongside Cosmos SDK and Polkadot's Substrate), which made it a common choice for teams prioritizing quick EVM-compatible launches.
TRON
Financial Primitive
high-throughput, low-cost · major stablecoin settlement rail
Less prominent in DeFi or NFT activity than Ethereum or Solana, but moves an outsized share of global stablecoin volume — particularly USDT — because of its combination of speed and near-zero fees. Functions closer to payment-rail infrastructure than a general application platform in practice, whatever its original design intent.
Cardano (ADA)
Frontier Research
research-driven, peer-reviewed development process · Ouroboros Proof-of-Stake
Distinguished less by any single technical feature than by its development philosophy — protocol changes go through academic peer review before implementation, a deliberately slower and more conservative process than most of its competitors. Has maintained a consistent top-10 market position for years despite this slower cadence, on the strength of that methodological reputation.
ZONE 2

Scaling & Modular Infrastructure

Ethereum's own base layer settled on a rollup-centric roadmap years ago: keep the base chain as the security and data-availability anchor, push actual execution up onto Layer 2s. Everything in this zone is a piece of that stack — the rollups that execute transactions, the restaking and data-availability layers that secure and feed them cheaply, and the messaging protocols that let all of it (and the non-Ethereum chains from Zone 1) actually talk to each other. This is the fastest-moving zone on the whole map — L2BEAT alone tracks 70+ active rollups — so what's below is the handful that structurally define the space, not an exhaustive list.

Rollups — Optimistic & Zero-Knowledge

L2BEAT tracked ~73 active rollups holding a combined ~$48B TVL as of April 2026 · optimistic rollups hold roughly 80% of that, ZK rollups the remaining ~20%
Arbitrum One
Scaling / Performance
Offchain Labs · launched Aug 2021 · optimistic rollup · L2BEAT Stage 1 (permissionless fraud proofs)
The largest Ethereum L2 by DeFi value locked, holding roughly $15–17B and commanding well over a third of all L2 DeFi market share on most 2026 estimates. Its BOLD fraud-proof upgrade and Timeboost sequencer-auction mechanism were both built specifically to close the decentralization gap between it and a truly trustless rollup, and a multi-party sequencer is slated for later in 2026. Deep, composable liquidity is the actual moat here — traders can route, hedge, and collateralize without ever leaving the chain, which is why capital concentrates on Arbitrum even when raw throughput or fees aren't the best in class.
Base
Scaling / Performance
built on the OP Stack, incubated by Coinbase · no native token · optimistic rollup, Stage 1
The busiest execution venue in the entire L2 landscape by transaction count — around 89 sustained TPS and roughly 380,000 daily active users as of April 2026, the first L2 to sustain daily transaction volume above Ethereum mainnet itself. That volume is a distribution story more than a DeFi-depth one: direct integration with Coinbase's user base gives Base the lowest onboarding friction of any major L2, which is precisely why consumer apps default to building here over Arbitrum's deeper but less accessible liquidity.
OP Mainnet + the Superchain
Scaling / Performance
OP Labs · ~$5–6B TVL · the OP Stack also underlies Base, World Chain, and dozens of other chains
OP Mainnet's own TVL trails Arbitrum and Base, but the actual strategic bet is different: the OP Stack is meant to be the shared, open-source chassis that many chains run on, coordinated through a "Superchain" vision of shared sequencing (in progress, via partners like Espresso) and shared governance. Every Base or World Chain transaction is, in a structural sense, a vote of confidence in this architecture — Optimism is playing an ecosystem-breadth game rather than competing purely on its own chain's liquidity depth.
zkSync Era
Frontier Research
Matter Labs · ZK rollup, full EVM compatibility · ~$3–4B TVL · L2BEAT Stage 0
Zero-knowledge rollups post cryptographic validity proofs rather than relying on a fraud-proof challenge window, which is why zkSync withdrawals to Ethereum settle in roughly 24 hours against the ~7-day wait optimistic rollups like Arbitrum require. Full EVM compatibility means existing Solidity contracts deploy with minimal changes — a real advantage over Starknet's separate language — but zkSync still trails the optimistic incumbents badly on both TVL and decentralization stage, and proof generation across the whole ZK-rollup category still leans on centralized or narrowly-distributed prover infrastructure as of 2026.
Starknet
Frontier Research
StarkWare · ZK rollup, Cairo language (not EVM) · ~$0.6–1.5B TVL
Uses STARK proofs and its own Cairo language rather than the EVM, a real technical divergence that suits compute-intensive workloads — large NFT mints, batched-verification DeFi primitives — but keeps its developer tooling separate from every EVM chain covered so far. Smallest TVL of the five rollups here, which is the direct cost of that non-EVM choice: technical differentiation without the drop-in compatibility that made zkSync and the optimistic rollups easy migration targets.

Restaking & Data Availability

the layer that decides how rollups get cheap, secure storage for the transaction data they post back to a base chain
EigenLayer
Core Infra
restaking protocol on Ethereum · crossed $18–19.5B in restaked ETH across ~1,900 operators by early 2026
Solves a specific bootstrapping problem: any new network or service historically had to recruit and pay its own independent validator set from zero. EigenLayer lets Ethereum stakers "restake" their already-staked ETH to simultaneously secure additional services — Actively Validated Services, or AVSs — inheriting Ethereum's existing economic security instead of starting from scratch. EigenDA, its own data-availability AVS, is the flagship use case and now processes data for multiple L2s at a claimed 100MB/s throughput. The 2026 story inside EigenLayer is specialization: a shift from generic shared security toward purpose-built "Vertical AVS" work, particularly around decentralized AI verification and cross-chain proof systems — directly adjacent to Zone 6 of this map.
Celestia
Core Infra
independent modular data-availability network · own validator set and token (TIA) · data availability sampling (DAS)
The sovereign alternative to EigenDA: Celestia separates consensus and data availability from execution entirely, running its own independent chain rather than inheriting Ethereum's validator set. Its light-node data-availability sampling lets nodes verify that data is available without downloading the entire dataset — the specific innovation that makes "modular" blockchain design practical at scale. The trade-off against EigenDA is the mirror image of Cosmos vs. Polkadot in Zone 1: full sovereignty and no Ethereum dependency, against the work of attracting and trusting an entirely separate validator set.

Interoperability & Cross-Chain Messaging

how a message, a token, or a proof actually moves from one chain to another — the layer where roughly 40% of all value ever stolen from crypto has been lost to exploits
Chainlink CCIP
Institutional / Regulatory
live since July 2023 · connects 50+ chains · three-network architecture with an independent Risk Management Network
Built explicitly for institutions rather than retail — a separate Risk Management Network monitors the messaging and token-transfer networks for anomalies, an extra verification layer most competing bridges don't run. That institutional design is winning real share in 2026: BitGo named CCIP its exclusive cross-chain provider in August (covering $7.7B+ of Wrapped Bitcoin), Kraken adopted it as its standard in May, and over $7.2B in cross-chain assets migrated from LayerZero to CCIP between May and July following a bridge exploit tied to Kelp DAO's rsETH token. The clearest current case of a security incident directly reshaping which infrastructure the largest institutional players trust.
LayerZero
Tooling / Dev Infra
omnichain messaging protocol · 90+ chains supported · configurable Decentralized Verifier Networks (DVNs)
Still the highest-volume cross-chain messaging protocol by raw share, historically processing the large majority of daily bridge messages across 130+ chains. Its OApp/OFT/ONFT standards became a genuine developer-adoption moat — lightweight, configurable trust assumptions, and broad chain support. 2026 has been a harder year for its institutional reputation specifically: the North Korea-linked Lazarus Group targeted its internal RPC infrastructure, and the Kelp DAO exploit (a vulnerability in a bridge built on top of LayerZero, not a flaw in LayerZero's core protocol itself) still triggered the institutional migration toward CCIP described above — a reminder that in this zone, being the biggest doesn't insulate a protocol from a single high-profile incident moving billions of dollars of trust elsewhere within weeks.
Wormhole
Tooling / Dev Infra
19 Guardian validator signers · $60B+ lifetime bridged volume · deepest Solana integration of any messaging protocol
The protocol most tightly integrated with Solana specifically, which matters given Solana's position as the default venue for high-frequency retail flow described in Zone 1 — Wormhole is frequently the actual pipe that flow uses to reach other ecosystems. Survived a $326M exploit in its earlier history, a scar that shaped its current Guardian-network security model. Uniswap DAO named it the only cross-chain protocol it unconditionally approved for integration, a notable trust signal from one of DeFi's largest and most conservative governance bodies.
ZONE 3

DeFi & Financial Primitives

The layer where blockchain infrastructure actually gets used for something: trading, lending, and the dollar-pegged instruments that make both possible. Decentralized exchanges alone moved roughly $4.9 trillion in spot volume plus another $8 trillion in perpetual-futures volume across 2025 — one in five crypto trades now happens without a centralized exchange in the middle. Structural facts about scale and mechanics below, not trading signals — none of this is investment advice.

Decentralized Exchanges (DEXs)

spot and perpetual-futures trading without a custodial intermediary · DEX-to-CEX spot ratio hit an all-time high of ~21% in late 2025
Uniswap
Financial Primitive
largest spot DEX by volume since 2020 · v4 introduced a "singleton" contract architecture to cut gas costs per swap
The reference implementation of the automated market maker (AMM) — liquidity providers deposit pairs of tokens into a shared pool rather than posting individual buy/sell orders, and price is set algorithmically by the pool's ratio. Concentrated-liquidity pools (introduced in v3, refined in v4) let LPs commit capital to a specific price range instead of the full curve, a major capital-efficiency gain over the original constant-product model. Still the default first stop for EVM spot trading even as newer entrants have captured larger raw volume elsewhere.
PancakeSwap
Financial Primitive
BNB Chain's dominant DEX · hit $325B in monthly volume in June 2025, its highest month ever
Built on the same concentrated-liquidity AMM model as Uniswap but tuned for BNB Chain's low-fee environment, which made it the default venue for high-frequency retail and memecoin flow. Briefly became the single largest DEX by quarterly volume in 2025 Q2, capturing an estimated 45% of all DEX trades that quarter — a reminder that "largest DEX" is a moving target that swings hard with whichever speculative cycle is peaking on whichever chain.
Hyperliquid
Financial Primitive
runs its own purpose-built L1, not deployed on top of another chain · fully on-chain central limit order book, not an AMM
The clearest structural break from the AMM model in this zone: Hyperliquid built its own chain specifically to support a traditional order-book matching engine on-chain, capable of roughly 200,000 orders per second per its own documentation. Captures an estimated 60–70% of all on-chain perpetual-futures volume as of 2026 — on January 26, 2026 it reportedly surpassed Binance itself on BTC perpetual liquidity depth. A multi-asset deleveraging cascade on October 10, 2025 pushed roughly $10 billion in liquidations through the venue in a single day, a reminder that deep on-chain liquidity doesn't remove the leverage risk underneath it.
Raydium + Jupiter
Financial Primitive
Raydium: Solana's leading AMM · Jupiter: Solana's dominant DEX aggregator, routing across all of Solana's liquidity sources
Solana flipped Ethereum in monthly DEX volume in January 2026 — $117B versus $52B — driven substantially by memecoin issuance through Pump.fun and PumpSwap feeding directly into Raydium's pools. Jupiter sits a layer above as an aggregator, splitting a single trade across multiple Solana AMMs to find the best combined price rather than running its own pool — the same functional role 1inch plays across EVM chains.

Lending & Credit Markets

second-largest DeFi category by TVL after liquid staking · 380+ active lending protocols tracked across 80+ chains, top 10 capture ~78% of deposits · roughly 84% of outstanding on-chain debt is stablecoin-denominated
Aave
Financial Primitive
largest on-chain lending protocol by a wide margin, cited TVL in the $15–27B range depending on the reporting date and methodology · deployed across 22+ networks
Non-custodial, overcollateralized lending — suppliers deposit an asset and receive an interest-bearing receipt token (aUSDC, aETH, etc.), borrowers post collateral above the loan value and get liquidated automatically if that collateral's value falls too far. e-Mode allows correlated-asset pairs (stablecoin-against-stablecoin, ETH-against-liquid-staking-tokens) to borrow at much higher loan-to-value ratios than uncorrelated collateral could safely support. Aave Horizon, launched 2025, extended the same mechanics to institutions borrowing against real-world assets, with VanEck, WisdomTree, and Securitize among the first participants — the clearest current bridge between DeFi lending mechanics and traditional asset managers.
Morpho
Financial Primitive
Morpho Blue: isolated lending-market primitive · curated "MetaMorpho" vaults let third parties build risk-managed products on top
Rather than one shared pool per asset like Aave, Morpho Blue lets anyone permissionlessly create an isolated lending market with its own collateral asset, loan asset, and risk parameters — curators then build vaults on top that route depositor funds across a chosen set of these markets according to their own risk mandate. Effectively separates the base lending primitive from the risk-curation layer, which had previously been bundled together in protocols like Aave and Compound.

Stablecoins & Regulation

total dollar-pegged stablecoin supply crossed $300B in early 2026 · annual transaction volume hit $33T in 2025, a 72% year-on-year increase · the GENIUS Act (signed July 2025) is the first federal US stablecoin law, with primary rules due and enforcement starting January 18, 2027
Tether (USDT)
Institutional / Regulatory
largest stablecoin, roughly 59% of total supply and ~74% of on-chain trading volume · issued from El Salvador, not the US
The dominant settlement asset for cross-border and emerging-market crypto activity, and — per its own attestations — a Treasury holding large enough to place Tether in a band with mid-sized sovereign holders on US Treasury International Capital tables. Its offshore domicile is now its central regulatory liability: the GENIUS Act requires a Treasury "reciprocity determination" for foreign issuers before they can keep serving US venues, which as of mid-2026 had not been issued for Tether. The company's response has been to launch USAT, a separate US-domiciled, GENIUS-compliant token, rather than restructure USDT itself — an unresolved fork in its own product line going into the law's January 2027 enforcement date.
Circle (USDC)
Institutional / Regulatory
2nd-largest stablecoin · issued by a US entity, MiCA-authorized in the EU via a French e-money license · leads by annual transaction volume ($18.3T vs. USDT's $13.3T in 2025)
Built its entire market position around being the compliant counterpart to Tether — monthly reserve attestations from a Big Four accounting firm, a US domicile that satisfies GENIUS Act requirements without a reciprocity determination, and EU market access Tether lost when it was delisted from Coinbase, Binance, Kraken, and Crypto.com's EU venues between December 2024 and March 2025 for lacking MiCA authorization. Circle separately secured approval for a national trust bank in 2026. Whether that compliance-first bet pays off in raw market share against Tether's dominant liquidity and network effects is the single biggest open question in this whole category heading into 2027.
ZONE 4

Frontier AI Labs

The model layer — who's actually training the systems everything else in AI gets built on top of. As of August 2026 the frontier has widened from three labs to effectively seven or eight: the original OpenAI/Anthropic/Google trio, joined by xAI and a returning Meta, plus a Chinese open-weight cluster (DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Moonshot's Kimi, Zhipu's GLM) now trailing the closed frontier by a matter of months rather than generations. New flagships ship roughly every 6–12 months per lab, with point updates landing almost monthly — treat every benchmark number below as a snapshot, not a settled ranking.
OpenAI
Frontier Research
flagship line: GPT-5.6, split into Sol / Terra / Luna tiers, GA July 2026 · Luna became the free ChatGPT default in August 2026
The lab that set the pace of the whole category after ChatGPT's 2022 public launch. GPT-5.6 restructured what used to be a single flagship into three tiers by task cost — Luna for fast exploratory work, Terra for most reversible production tasks, Sol for planning and high-stakes review — reportedly the first frontier model to clear a customer-by-customer US government review before public release, and benchmarked as emitting under half the output tokens per coding task of comparable competitor models on independent evaluation suites.
Anthropic
Frontier Research
Claude 5 family: Opus 5 (Jul 24, 2026), Fable 5 / Mythos 5 (Jun 9, 2026), Sonnet 5 (Jun 30, 2026) · Series H valuation ~$965B post-money (May 2026)
Fable 5 and Mythos 5 were briefly suspended June 12–30, 2026 under a US export-control directive before being restored to general availability — a rare case of a geopolitical policy action directly interrupting a frontier model's public access mid-deployment. Opus 5 followed a month later, landing within half a percent of Fable 5's coding-benchmark performance at roughly half the inference cost. Anthropic's valuation trajectory — $61.5B in May 2025 to ~$965B by May 2026 — has been among the steepest of any company in the category.
Google DeepMind
Frontier Research
Gemini 3.6 Flash (stable GA Jul 21, 2026) · leadership change: Koray Kavukcuoglu named to lead the unit in Aug 2026
Gemini 3, released November 2025, was widely described as moving the frontier forward on release — but subsequent 2026 point releases haven't repeated that jump while OpenAI and Anthropic both shipped major new flagships, leaving Google in a catch-up position by mid-2026 despite still leading specific benchmarks like GPQA Diamond on some model generations. An internal "Code Strike" team was formed specifically to close the coding-capability gap, framed internally around the broader race toward AGI. Sister company Isomorphic Labs, applying the same underlying research to drug discovery, raised $600M separately in early 2025.
xAI
Frontier Research
Grok 4.6 (released Aug 12, 2026) · positioned as the lean, agentic, cost-conscious option among the four Western frontier labs
Grok's mid-2026 jump (4.5, boosted by a SpaceX/Cursor integration) was specifically described as reaching Opus-class performance at a lower price point — the clearest example in this zone of a lab competing on cost-efficiency rather than trying to out-benchmark the leaders outright. xAI had promised to open-source Grok 3's weights; as of August 2026 those weights still hadn't been published, a gap between announced and delivered openness worth noting given how central "open" positioning is to the category's competitive narrative.
Meta AI
Frontier Research
Muse Spark 1.2 (closed, released Aug 5, 2026) · Llama 4 (Behemoth, Maverick, Scout) remains open-weight
A genuine strategic reversal: Meta built its entire prior reputation on open-weight Llama releases, then launched Muse Spark as a closed, API-only flagship priced aggressively below the other closed labs — while keeping the Llama line open alongside it. That split leaves Meta running both playbooks simultaneously, and effectively ceded the "most competitive open-weight lab" title it once held outright to the Chinese cluster below.
Mistral AI
Frontier Research
Mistral Medium 3.5 (released Apr 28, 2026) · Paris-based, Europe's leading frontier-adjacent lab
Positioned specifically as the European champion — strong multilingual and European-language performance where the mostly English-benchmarked US and Chinese models are comparatively weaker. Runs a genuine dual strategy like Meta: commercial flagship tiers (Mistral Large) alongside Apache 2.0-licensed open releases (Mixtral, Codestral, the Ministral edge-device line) that punch well above their parameter count on cost-to-quality.
DeepSeek
Frontier Research
DeepSeek-V4-Pro (released Apr 24, 2026) · owned by High-Flyer Capital, a Chinese quant hedge fund · original V3 reportedly trained for around $6M
The efficiency story that reset the entire category's assumptions about training cost — V3's reported ~$6M training budget matched GPT-4-class quality on many benchmarks at a small fraction of what Western labs were reporting for comparable runs, regardless of how that figure is ultimately reconciled against total compute spend. By 2026, DeepSeek's own estimate is that its open weights trail the closed frontier by only a few months on many benchmarks — a gap that keeps shrinking rather than holding steady.
Alibaba (Qwen)
Frontier Research
Qwen3.8-Max (released Aug 3, 2026) · Qwen3.6-27B runs competitively on a single consumer GPU
Alibaba's Qwen line spans from edge-deployable small models to frontier-scale flagships in one family, giving it a breadth few other labs match — the same series that produces a 27B model competitive on SWE-bench also produces the Max-tier flagship competing directly with DeepSeek and the Western frontier. Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 (2.8 trillion parameters, the largest open-weights release to date as of mid-2026) and Zhipu AI's GLM-5 series round out the wider Chinese open-weight cluster, together making Chinese labs the dominant source of frontier-class open weights in 2026, a position the US open-weight ecosystem (Llama, OpenAI's gpt-oss) has not matched at the same pace.
ZONE 5

AI Compute & Infrastructure

The layer underneath every model in Zone 4 — the silicon that trains and serves them, and the data centers that rent out access to that silicon. Nvidia still holds roughly 70–80% of data-center AI accelerator revenue depending on the estimate, but that figure is a shrinking share of a market that's roughly doubled year over year — hyperscaler custom silicon is growing at ~44.6% CAGR against ~16% for merchant GPUs. Every figure here is a snapshot from a market moving month to month, not a settled ranking, and none of it is investment advice.

Silicon

combined hyperscaler capex for AI infrastructure: $660–690B in 2026 · nearly every custom chip in this category fabricates on TSMC's 3nm node, which runs at full capacity with demand roughly 3x supply
Nvidia
Tooling / Dev Infra
~70–80% of data-center AI accelerator revenue (estimates vary by source and quarter) · FY2026 data-center revenue reported around $193.7B · next-gen Vera Rubin platform: 50 PFLOPS FP4, 288GB HBM4
The default supplier for nearly every lab in Zone 4 — CUDA's software ecosystem remains the deepest moat in the category, which is why even hyperscalers racing to build their own chips (below) remain simultaneously Nvidia's largest customers. Analysts project Nvidia's share of the inference market specifically — as distinct from training, where its lead is more durable — could fall from over 90% toward 20–30% by 2028 as custom ASICs absorb the two-thirds of AI compute that inference now represents. Not a prediction of Nvidia's revenue shrinking — the addressable market is growing fast enough that even a smaller slice is a larger absolute number than today.
AMD
Tooling / Dev Infra
~5–8% of data-center AI accelerator revenue · Instinct MI350X line, positioned directly against Nvidia's B200
The clearest like-for-like Nvidia alternative on raw specs — MI350X reportedly matches B200 on FP8 throughput — but adoption lags specs because CUDA's software and tooling ecosystem doesn't have a true equivalent on AMD's ROCm stack yet. Runs a distant second to Nvidia by revenue, and a distant third to the combined hyperscaler custom-silicon category by growth rate.
Hyperscaler Custom Silicon (TPU / Trainium / Maia / MTIA)
Tooling / Dev Infra
Google TPU v7 "Ironwood," Amazon Trainium 3, Microsoft Maia 200, Meta MTIA — combined ~15–20% of accelerator revenue, growing ~44.6% CAGR
Four hyperscalers building application-specific chips for their own captive workloads rather than selling a branded product on the open market — you can't buy a TPU, you rent access to one through Google Cloud. Google's program is the most mature, co-developed with Broadcom and MediaTek, and projects 4.3 million TPU shipments in 2026; Anthropic alone committed to up to one million TPUs in what was reported as the largest deal in Google Cloud's history. Amazon disclosed over $225B in revenue commitments for Trainium as of its Q1 2026 earnings call. The strategic logic across all four is the same: every workload moved off a rented Nvidia GPU and onto in-house silicon keeps more margin inside the company that owns the chip.
Cerebras
Tooling / Dev Infra
wafer-scale engine (WSE-3) — an entire silicon wafer as one chip, architecturally distinct from every GPU in this zone · IPO'd Nasdaq May 14, 2026, raising $5.55B, the largest US tech IPO since Uber in 2019
Where GPU-based inference providers compete on clever scheduling across many small chips, Cerebras competes by making the chip itself enormous — a full silicon wafer instead of chips cut from one, avoiding the interconnect bottleneck between chips entirely. Reports roughly 2,000–3,000 tokens/second on models like Llama Scout and gpt-oss-120B, among the fastest raw inference numbers publicly benchmarked in the category, at the cost of a narrower supported-model catalog than general-purpose GPU clouds.
Broadcom
Tooling / Dev Infra
$73B AI backlog · targeting ~$100B in annual AI chip revenue by 2027 · AI semiconductor revenue hit $10.8B in a single fiscal 2026 quarter, up 143% year-on-year
Doesn't sell a chip under its own name, which is exactly why it rarely appears on "Nvidia alternative" lists despite arguably being the second most important company in the entire AI chip supply chain after Nvidia and TSMC — Broadcom is the custom-silicon architect that Google and Meta actually design their ASICs with before TSMC fabricates them. Marvell plays the equivalent role for Amazon's Trainium and Microsoft's Maia, projecting up to $11B in AI ASIC revenue for 2026 — a smaller but growing second option in the same design-partner niche.

Cloud & "Neocloud" Infrastructure

neocloud category-wide revenue: ~$12B/quarter as of mid-2026, a ~$48B annualized run-rate · H100 rental rates run 40–85% below equivalent hyperscaler pricing depending on provider and contract term
CoreWeave
Modern-Built
largest independent "neocloud" · FY2025 revenue $5.1B (+168% YoY), 2026 guidance $12–13B, contracted backlog $99.4B · Nasdaq-listed since March 2025 (CRWV)
Rents Nvidia GPU clusters at prices hyperscalers structurally can't match, undercutting AWS and Azure by roughly 40–85% depending on the comparison — the arbitrage works because CoreWeave built bare-metal, AI-native infrastructure from scratch rather than retrofitting GPU instances onto a general-purpose cloud platform. Nine of the ten leading AI model providers reportedly run workloads on it, including a April 2026 multiyear deal to run Claude at production scale and a $21B capacity commitment from Meta running through 2032. The other side of that scale: $11.2B in high-yield debt secured against GPUs that depreciate every time Nvidia ships a new generation, with billions due for refinancing through 2026 — a genuine leverage risk sitting underneath the growth story.
Lambda + RunPod + the wider neocloud field
Modern-Built
Lambda: preparing an IPO after a Microsoft deal and $1.5B in funding · RunPod, Vast.ai, Nebius, Fluidstack round out the category's next tier
Behind CoreWeave sits a second tier of GPU-first clouds running the same basic playbook at smaller scale — Lambda building its own AI-factory capacity in Kansas City, RunPod and Vast.ai competing more on marketplace-style spot pricing for smaller teams. Fluidstack is the extreme case of the category's asset-light model: it owns none of its data center buildings, with Google guaranteeing the lease obligations so landlords could borrow against them — infrastructure that's contractually real but ultimately backed by a hyperscaler's balance sheet rather than the neocloud's own.

Specialized Inference Platforms

the layer that serves open-weight models (Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM) via API rather than training them — distinct from both the chip layer and the labs in Zone 4
Groq
Modern-Built
custom LPU (Language Processing Unit) chips, not GPUs · 400–800+ tokens/second on Llama-class models · raised $650M in June 2026 to scale its inference cloud, alongside a non-exclusive Nvidia licensing deal announced December 2025
Wins on raw speed by storing model weights directly in on-chip SRAM instead of using it as cache, removing the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that caps GPU inference speed — the trade-off is a narrow catalog, roughly 20 models, versus the hundreds available on GPU-based competitors. The Nvidia licensing deal, folded into Nvidia's own next-generation LPX platform announced at GTC, is a notable case of the dominant incumbent licensing a challenger's core technology rather than only competing against it.
Together AI + Fireworks AI
Modern-Built
Together: 100–200+ open models hosted, broadest catalog in the category · Fireworks: built by ex-Meta PyTorch engineers, $250M Series C at a $4B valuation, 99.8% reported uptime
Where Groq wins on speed within a narrow catalog, these two compete on breadth and production reliability across a much wider one — Together as the "try any open model" default for research and experimentation, Fireworks as the production-grade choice for teams that need function calling, structured outputs, and enterprise SLAs on frontier-scale open weights like DeepSeek V4 and Kimi K3 that Groq doesn't carry. All three, plus DeepInfra and Baseten, expose OpenAI-compatible endpoints, which means switching between them is typically a one-line base-URL change rather than a real migration.
ZONE 6

Convergence — Crypto × AI

The zone this whole map has been building toward — where blockchain infrastructure and AI actually meet, rather than just sitting next to each other in the same news cycle. Two real problems drive it: AI compute is scarce and controlled by a handful of companies (Zone 5), and AI agents need a way to hold money and transact without a human clicking "approve" every time. Everything below is either renting out spare compute through a blockchain-coordinated marketplace, or giving an AI agent an on-chain identity and wallet. Market-cap and revenue figures move week to week in this category more than any other zone on this map — treat every number as a snapshot, not a ranking, and none of it as investment advice.

Decentralized Compute Networks

the core pitch: GPU chips sitting idle anywhere in the world can be rented out through a blockchain-coordinated marketplace, usually 40–90% cheaper than AWS/Azure list price — the decentralized-GPU sector collectively ran an estimated $200M in annualized protocol revenue in early 2026
Bittensor (TAO)
Convergence
organized into independent "subnets," each built around one AI task (text generation, image recognition, price prediction, and dozens more) · closer to a decentralized intelligence marketplace than a pure GPU rental network
The mechanism worth understanding: each subnet runs its own competition where participants submit AI work and get paid in TAO based on how good that work is, judged by the subnet's own validators — a market that pays for machine intelligence output directly, rather than paying for raw compute hours the way the networks below do. That makes it structurally closer to Zone 4's model layer than to Zone 5's chip-rental layer, even though it's usually grouped with the compute networks below.
Render Network + Akash Network
Convergence
Render: originated in 3D/visual-effects rendering, now expanded into AI inference, reported monthly revenue around $38M · Akash: general-purpose "supercloud" for container workloads, recorded a $5M compute-spend quarter in Q1 2026
Render's edge is a head start — it already had a real base of paying creative-industry customers before AI demand arrived, and simply pointed the same idle-GPU marketplace at inference workloads too. Akash takes the more general-purpose path: instead of specializing in GPUs, it rents out any container workload, which has made it a known hosting venue in 2026 specifically for open-weight and "uncensored" language models that mainstream cloud providers won't host under their own usage policies — a real product-market fit that's also a genuine content-moderation trade-off, not a neutral technical detail.
io.net + Gensyn
Convergence
io.net: aggregates GPUs from Render, Akash, and independent sources into unified rentable clusters, claims access to 300,000+ GPUs across 55+ countries · Gensyn: a16z-backed, focused specifically on distributed training rather than inference, using a "proof-of-learning" mechanism to verify the work was actually done correctly
io.net solves a coordination problem — rather than being its own compute source, it's a layer that stitches together capacity from several networks (including some already listed above) into one rentable unit, letting a developer rent the equivalent of 1,000 H100s as a single virtual cluster even though the underlying hardware sits with dozens of separate operators. Gensyn tackles a harder, less-solved problem: training a model requires many machines to stay in tight sync, which is difficult to verify honestly across untrusted, geographically scattered hardware — proof-of-learning is its attempt at a cryptographic answer to "how do we know this remote GPU actually did the training work it claims."

On-Chain AI Agents

the other half of the convergence: giving an autonomous AI agent its own wallet, identity, and ability to transact — Virtuals-launched agent tokens alone number in the thousands, most of them speculative and short-lived
Virtuals Protocol
Convergence
built on Base · dominant agent launchpad, has enabled roughly 14,000 agent-token launches since inception · AIXBT is its best-known individual agent, focused on token/market analysis
The core idea: anyone can spin up an AI agent with a defined persona and task, then issue a token tied to that specific agent's performance — effectively an IPO mechanism for a single AI bot rather than a company. A small number of agents (AIXBT among them) have shown real, sustained activity and following; the large majority of the roughly 14,000 launched tokens follow the same fast-pump, fast-fail pattern as memecoins, because the underlying mechanism — permissionless token launch with no vetting — is structurally the same one memecoin platforms use.
Artificial Superintelligence Alliance (Fetch.ai + SingularityNET)
Convergence
formed 2024 by merging Fetch.ai, SingularityNET, and Ocean Protocol under a shared FET token · Ocean Protocol withdrew from the merger in October 2025 to control its own tokenomics separately
Three different pieces stitched into one alliance: Fetch.ai contributed tooling for agents that can autonomously negotiate and transact (its original use cases were logistics and travel booking), SingularityNET runs a marketplace where developers list AI models/services and get paid per use, and Ocean Protocol (before it left) supplied the data-sharing rails those models would train on. The Ocean Protocol exit a year and a half into the merger is worth noting on its own — a concrete example of how difficult aligning tokenomics and incentives across merged crypto projects actually is in practice, even when the underlying technical rationale for combining them still makes sense.
ai16z + ElizaOS
Convergence
an AI-managed investment DAO — the ai16z token grants governance and profit-sharing rights over decisions an AI agent makes · ElizaOS is the open-source agent framework the project publishes and that others have built on
The most literal expression of "AI agent with financial autonomy" in this whole zone: rather than a human fund manager making calls that token holders vote to ratify, the stated design lets an AI agent make investment decisions directly, with token holders sharing in the outcome. Together with Virtuals, it holds a majority of the whole AI-agent token category's combined value — a concentration that means the category's health currently rides heavily on just these two projects continuing to execute, not on a broad, diversified field of credible competitors.
ZONE 7

Institutions, Regulation & Capital

The final zone — who sets the rules everything in Zones 1–6 has to operate inside, who provides regulated access to it for people who'll never touch a wallet directly, and who funds what gets built next. Two regulatory regimes now shape the whole category more than any other force: the US SEC under a changed leadership posture, and the EU's MiCA framework, which went into full enforcement on July 1, 2026 and has already reshuffled which exchanges can legally serve European users. Nothing in this zone is investment advice — figures are structural snapshots of a category that moves month to month.

Regulators

the two frameworks doing the most to shape market structure in 2026
US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)
Institutional / Regulatory
under Chair Paul Atkins since 2025, replacing Gary Gensler · streamlined listing standards have compressed crypto ETF approval timelines sharply
The leadership change is the single fact that explains most of the rest of this zone: Gensler's SEC had instructed issuers to strip staking components out of ETF filings; under Atkins, BlackRock's staked-Ethereum ETF (ETHB) cleared without objection, and Bloomberg Intelligence tracked over 100 additional crypto ETF filings pending by early 2026 under the faster process. In August 2026 the agency also proposed new exemptions letting certain crypto investment contracts raise up to $5M over four years, or $75M annually with added disclosure — a meaningfully lighter fundraising path than a traditional securities registration.
MiCA (EU Markets in Crypto-Assets)
Institutional / Regulatory
full enforcement across all 27 EU states from July 1, 2026 · a single "passportable" license covers all 27 countries · only 244 firms held CASP authorization at the grandfathering deadline
The clearest test case anywhere in this zone of regulation directly reordering a market: Binance withdrew its Greek MiCA license application on June 24, 2026, just six days before the deadline, temporarily locking itself out of licensed EU service while Coinbase, Kraken, OKX, and Crypto.com — all already licensed — absorbed the users it couldn't legally keep serving. Kraken ended up with the largest liquidity pool of any licensed EU exchange in the deadline's immediate aftermath. National regulators are now running risk-based inspections of licensed firms' governance, key custody, and smart-contract risk through mid-2027, feeding into a consolidated report for ESMA — meaning the current licensing outcome is a snapshot, not the end state.

Regulated Access — ETFs

US spot crypto ETFs crossed $2 trillion in cumulative trading volume in January 2026, roughly two years after the first spot Bitcoin ETF launched — the second trillion took half as long to reach as the first
BlackRock (IBIT + ETHA/ETHB)
Institutional / Regulatory
IBIT: roughly 70% share of spot Bitcoin ETF trading volume, tens of billions in AUM · ETHB (staked Ethereum ETF) launched March 2026, splits staking yield 82/18 with Coinbase as custodian
The single most concentrated point of institutional crypto exposure in the entire market — IBIT alone briefly made BlackRock the largest known holder of Bitcoin globally in April 2026 (a position Strategy, formerly MicroStrategy, retook later that year through continued direct accumulation). ETHB's staking-yield structure was only possible because of two things landing in sequence: the GENIUS Act clearing regulatory ambiguity around yield-bearing crypto products, and the Atkins-era SEC dropping Gensler's staking objection — a direct chain from Zone 3's stablecoin law to a specific retail-accessible product.
The Wider ETF Field
Institutional / Regulatory
2025 net inflows: ~$21.8B into Bitcoin ETFs, ~$9.8B into Ethereum ETFs · August 2025 was the first month Ethereum ETF inflows exceeded Bitcoin's · altcoin ETFs now cover Solana, XRP, Dogecoin, and Chainlink, plus multi-asset index products like Grayscale's GDLC
Demand remains heavily concentrated in Bitcoin and Ethereum products even as the number of available tickers has multiplied — Bloomberg Intelligence's own analyst has flagged that many of the 100+ pending altcoin ETF filings are likely to launch into thin, unsustained demand and eventually close, a direct tension between "more regulated access exists now" and "not all of it will find a durable audience."

Exchanges

where regulated fiat-to-crypto access actually happens for most users — the layer this whole map ultimately has to pass through to reach anyone not already holding crypto
Coinbase
Institutional / Regulatory
publicly listed (Nasdaq: COIN) · MiCA-licensed across the EU · custodian for BlackRock's ETHB staking product
The exchange that most consistently positioned itself as the compliance-first option ahead of major regulatory deadlines — a strategy that paid off directly when Binance's Greek MiCA withdrawal in June 2026 sent EU users looking for an already-licensed alternative. Also sits structurally inside Zone 3 (as USDC's issuing partner alongside Circle) and Zone 5 (as an infrastructure counterparty), making it one of the few entities on this whole map with a foothold in three different zones at once.
Binance
Institutional / Regulatory
world's largest exchange by global volume · withdrew its Greek MiCA application June 24, 2026, six days ahead of the EU's hard deadline · roughly three-quarters of its user base sits in emerging markets, outside the EU
The clearest example in this entire map of a dominant incumbent losing ground to slower-moving but earlier-compliant competitors in one specific jurisdiction, while remaining largely insulated globally because of where the bulk of its actual users are. Publicly stated it will pursue MiCA authorization through a different EU member state, but had not named one as of the withdrawal — leaving a real gap between "committed to re-entering" and an actual licensed re-entry.
Kraken
Institutional / Regulatory
largest liquidity pool of any MiCA-licensed exchange as of July 2026 ($399.71M spot, $206.9M perpetual) · supports the widest market coverage of any licensed EU venue (1,704 markets)
Went into the MiCA deadline already licensed and came out the immediate liquidity leader in the region — the direct beneficiary, alongside Coinbase, of Binance's withdrawal. One of the exchanges named specifically in Chainlink CCIP's institutional adoption story back in Zone 2, another example of how thin the line is between "exchange" and "infrastructure counterparty" once an entity operates at this scale.

Capital — Crypto-Native Venture Funds

crypto-focused VC deployment ran near $5B in Q1 2026, down from ~$6B a year earlier — a cooling market even as individual funds got larger
a16z crypto
Institutional / Regulatory
closed a $2.2B fifth fund in May 2026, bringing total capital raised to $9.8B since 2018 · backed Coinbase, Solana Foundation, Uniswap, and Kalshi historically
Explicitly stayed "100% dedicated to crypto" for this fund even as its own peers — Paradigm, Polychain — were taking meetings with AI infrastructure startups in the same window, a deliberate contrarian bet articulated in its own announcement: opaque AI systems and consolidated internet infrastructure are exactly the conditions that make verifiable, decentralized crypto infrastructure more valuable, not less. Named stablecoins, tokenization, perpetual futures, prediction markets, and AI agents — several of them entities elsewhere on this exact map — as its priority areas.
Paradigm
Institutional / Regulatory
founded 2018 by Matt Huang (ex-Sequoia) and Fred Ehrsam (Coinbase co-founder) · manages roughly $12.7B across its funds · raised a new $1.2B vehicle in July 2026 explicitly covering AI and robotics alongside crypto
The direct counter-case to a16z crypto's staying-the-course bet: Paradigm is using its existing crypto-native technical team to evaluate AI and robotics deals rather than spinning up a separate practice, a bet that its deep protocol-design expertise transfers across categories rather than staying siloed in crypto. Co-led Morpho's $175M raise in June 2026 alongside a16z crypto — the same lending protocol covered in Zone 3 — one of several points on this map where two entities from entirely different zones turn out to be the same capital behind the scenes.
